A route suitable for a robot sharing space with people has to be short enough to be efficient, executable by the controller, and far enough from people, and my results show that no single measure captures all three. In Mission 1, BFS and A* both found the 13.5 m shortest path on the open map, but A* expanded 700 cells against 928 for BFS. DFS returned valid routes that were far too long, 111 m on the open map and 104 m against 24.5 m on the constrained map, and IDDFS reached the resource limit on both. In Mission 2 those differences became navigation outcomes. DFS timed out at 179.9 s after travelling 44.4 m with a mean tracking error of 7.14 m, while A* finished the constrained map in 89.5 s over 23.9 m. Nav2 was fastest on both maps, at 35.2 s open and 73.8 s constrained, and it kept 0.26 m of wall clearance against 0.11 m for my A*. In Mission 3 the field did not change either route, since the planned lengths were identical across conditions. A* passed the person at 0.742, 0.734 and 0.724 m for baseline, social_1 and social_2, and RRT passed at 1.706 to 1.735 m. The large RRT clearance came from its sampled route, not from the field. Two things were measured and not directly observed. First, the occupancy grid is an inflated model of the world, and Manhattan distance is only an estimate of the remaining route. Second, distance_m comes from AMCL, so it is an estimate and not ground truth, and the clearance numbers depend on the mapped geometry and a static person. Each cell was also a single trial, so none of this is a measured success rate.
