## concepts.prediction
Classical detection will fail suddenly and predictably, while learned detection will fade gradually but be harder to explain. Dim light, distance, and occlusion will mostly cause misses, the classical color mask breaks or the blob shrinks below the size threshold, while the learned detector just loses confidence. Glare will hurt both, since it washes out color and texture. Clutter and a similar looking distractor will mostly cause false detections, the classical detector accepts anything matching its color and shape rules, and the learned detector may confidently label a distractor it never saw in training. Rotation barely matters for a color blob but depends on training coverage for a learned model. Either way, the robot should treat detections as uncertain evidence.

## mission_1.limitations
The one parameter I changed that mattered most was the value maximum, which I lowered to 131. This removed the bright wood from the mask, so the mug no longer merged with the desk and the box shrank from the mug plus desk to just the mug. The limitation is that the rule only matches a color and brightness range and has no idea what a mug is. A different cup, such as a white or brightly colored one, could fall outside my range and be missed. A different background, such as dark or brown, could pass the same rule and win because it is bigger. The test results show this too: the box precision was only about 0.42, meaning many reported boxes were not on the cup.

## pillar_search.diagnosis
In the blue stage the pillar first appeared at the left edge of the camera image, with a normalized error of −0.98. The mask showed one clean white shape on the pillar, so the correct contour was selected and the centroid sat on it. A negative error means the target is on the left. The controller negates the error, so it commanded a positive angular velocity up to +0.45 rad/s, which turns the robot left. As the pillar moved toward the middle of the image, the error shrank to −0.12, and the turn command shrank with it. In the saved frame the centroid is just left of the center line about −0.06, which is inside the 0.10 tolerance. The robot then confirmed three centered frames and held still. I changed the steering gain from 1.2 to 0.8. It improved smoothness: no color crossed zero error, whereas at gain 1.2 green and turquoise overshot and ended on the right side of center, green at +0.07. It made the run slower, each centering phase was longer, and the total went from 18.99 s to 20.62 s. It also left the robot stopping nearer the edge of the tolerance −0.05 to −0.08 instead of close to 0, because the smaller commands near the target let it stop as soon as it enters the tolerance band.

## pillar_search.evidence
All four colors were centered (red, green, turquoise and blue), and none were missed. The state timeline shows each color went through SEARCH, CENTER, CONFIRM, HOLD and NEXT, with COMPLETE after blue. During SEARCH the robot rotated at a constant 0.35 rad/s until the pillar appeared, and each pillar entered from the left edge with an error near −1, so the controller commanded a positive angular velocity, a left turn, each time while the error shrank into the 0.10 tolerance. The camera evidence agrees: the mask is a single clean contour on the pillar, with the centroid close to the center line, and the linear speed was 0 throughout, so the robot never drove forward. However, a centered colored region and a stable hold do not prove object identity or distance. The controller only checks that the largest region in a color range sits near the image center, so a different object of a similar color would pass the same test and color is not identity. The robot only rotates, and the centroid’s horizontal position gives a direction rather than a range, so a small near pillar and a large far pillar can produce the same centered blob. A stable hold shows only that the error stayed within tolerance while the robot stood still, meaning the control loop is steady, and it says nothing about whether the target is the right object or where it is in the world.

## line_following.comparison
On the straight practice strip, which used a gain of 0.08, the robot tracked stably. The error started at about -0.37, shrank to roughly 0.00 by 40 s, and the turn command stayed tiny, under 0.03 rad/s. On the curved track, the first bend, from about 40 to 100 s, held the error at +0.6 to +0.8 for about a minute. The turn command had the correct sign but was only about -0.05 rad/s, far below the 0.6 rad/s limit. So the problem was under-correction, not oscillation: the error rarely changed sign, so there was no left-right swinging. The evidence for the gain change comes from comparing the two track runs. Gain 0.08 reached a stronger peak turn of 0.071 rad/s versus 0.050, and a lower peak error of 0.88 versus 0.99, and it completed the route at 187 s. Gain 0.05 had larger errors and lost the line at 160 s. This is only one run per gain, so the conclusion is suggestive rather than proven.

## line_following.limits
The gain 0.05 track run stopped in LINE_LOST at 160 s. Its error grew from -0.86 to -0.99, which means the line drifted to the left edge of the image and then left the camera region. The failure began in control, not image processing: the mask was correct until the line left the frame, and the steering was too weak for the sharp bend. It then became a missing-information problem, because the robot has no map, no lidar and no memory of where the line went, so stopping was its only safe option. A real delivery robot around people would need lidar or depth sensing to detect people and obstacles, bumpers and an emergency stop, and wheel odometry or an IMU to handle a lost line. It would be tested under varied lighting and glare, with a faded or occluded line, with people crossing its path while measuring stopping distance, with camera dropouts, and with repeated runs per setting to separate real effects from run-to-run variation.
